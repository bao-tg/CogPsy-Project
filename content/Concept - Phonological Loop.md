## **Phonological Loop (Baddeley & Hitch, 1974)**
### **Two main components**

1. **Phonological Store** (“inner ear”)
    - Holds sounds or speech-based info for about **1–2 seconds**.
    - **It _automatically decays_ unless refreshed.**
    - Example: You hear “9–4–2,” you can **keep it for a moment** before it fades.
2. **Articulatory Rehearsal Process** (“inner voice”)
    - You **silently repeat** the information to refresh it.
    - Example: You keep whispering “9–4–2” in your head so you don’t forget it.

Together, they prevent information from fading — the “loop” is you _rehearsing_ what’s stored in your “inner ear.”

### **Evidence**

- **Word Length Effect:** Short words (cat, dog, sun) are remembered better than long ones (university, refrigerator) — because they take less time to rehearse.
- **Articulatory Suppression:** If you repeat an irrelevant sound (“la la la”) while learning, your performance drops — because you disrupt the rehearsal loop.

## **Naveh-Benjamin & Ayres (1986): Word Length and Memory Span Across Languages**
### **Aim**
To test whether **differences in memory span across languages** are due to how long it takes to **pronounce words**, as predicted by the **phonological loop model** (Baddeley & Hitch, 1974).
### **Method**

Participants from **different language backgrounds** (e.g., English, Spanish, Hebrew, Arabic) were asked to recall **lists of digits or short words** in their native language.
### **Findings**

- Memory span was **larger for languages where words are spoken faster** (like English).    
- Memory span was **smaller for languages with longer word pronunciations** (like Arabic).
- The number of _items_ remembered depended on **time**, not just number of syllables.

# Angela M. AuBuchon & Rebecca L. Wagner (2023)
### What the study is

- They investigated how **strategy use** (especially self-generated, non-phonological strategies) influences performance when remembering lists of words that are **phonologically similar** (e.g. “cat, bat, mat”) versus **distinct** (e.g. “dog, pen, jar”)
- They collected **self-reports** of which strategies participants used (e.g. **rehearsal, imagery, sentence generation**). 

## Key *findings*

1. **Use of non-phonological strategies helps with similar materials**
    - When the lists were phonologically similar (harder to keep separate with rehearsal alone), participants often reported using **mental imagery** and **sentence generation** in addition to rehearsal.
    - These alternative strategies predicted better recall performance for the similar lists — more so than just relying on rehearsal.
2. **Strategy choice is influenced by context / order**
    - **When the phonologically similar lists came first (before easier, distinct lists), participants were more likely to adopt non-phonological strategy**
    - But if they first encountered distinct (easier) lists, they tended to stick with **phonological rehearsal** strategies even when later given similar (harder) lists.
3. **Rehearsal/verbalization not always the best predictor**
    - Use of verbal rehearsal or verbalization by itself did **not** reliably predict recall accuracy for similar lists.
    - Instead, participants who reported **mental imagery + sentence generation** (often in combination with rehearsal) had **higher memory accuracy** for the difficult (phonologically similar) lists.

## How this fits into theory (and your learning)

- Before, the **phonological loop theory** suggested that lists of phonetically similar items are harder because of **interference in the phonological store / rehearsal mechanism**.
- AuBuchon & Wagner’s work shows that people don’t passively suffer from that interference; some **actively adopt alternative strategies** to overcome it (e.g. visualization, semantic linking, sentence generation).
- This suggests that _strategy flexibility_ is a key moderator of working memory performance, especially under more difficult conditions.
- Also it highlights **individual differences**: not everyone will switch strategies, and prior experience or the order of tasks influences which strategy is used.
