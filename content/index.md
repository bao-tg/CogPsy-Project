# Rationale

**Context & Mechanism**: In modern academic and professional environments, multitasking is often viewed as a necessary skill for productivity. However, cognitive psychology research, specifically the Broadbent's filter model of attention or Filter theory of attention (Broadbent, 1958), suggests that the human working memory has a limited capacity for processing information, and  attentional system is serial rather than parallel when processing response selections. When individuals attempt to perform two attention-demanding tasks simultaneously, they experience a "cognitive switch cost" - a delay caused by the executive control processes required to disengage from one task rule set and engage another.

**The Problem (The "Gap")**: Despite the strong evidence for switch costs, many university students continue to study in environments rife with digital distractions, operating under the metacognitive illusion that they are "good multitaskers." While previous studies have established that multitasking degrades performance, there is a need to replicate these findings in a student-specific context to demonstrate the tangible loss in accuracy (error rate) that occurs during typical academic tasks.

**The Current Study**: Therefore, this project aims to quantify the cost of divided attention on task efficiency by comparing error rates between a single-task condition and a frequent-switching condition. This research is significant as it provides empirical evidence to challenge the popular student belief that multitasking is an efficient study strategy.
# Research Question

“Does multitasking-induced working memory load impair performance in cognitive-related tasks? To what extent does single-tasking versus divided attention affect the error rate of cognitively demanding tasks?”

# Hypothesis

Participants in the single-task condition will complete the assigned task with fewer errors than those in the switching condition.

# Methodology

## Participants

All of the students in the class. (n = 24)

## Research design

### Primary test: International Linguistic Olympiad (IOL)

**Source:** https://ioling.org/ 

**Information:** The test challenges participants to analyze the grammar, structure, culture, and history of different languages and to demonstrate their linguistic abilities through puzzles and problem-solving challenges. The ILO encourages creativity and imagination and helps participants develop skills in language analysis and problem-solving. Due to the time constraints of only 15 minutes, we had developed a new ILO-style test adapted from the original format, preserving its core objectives while aligning the structure to fit the available timeframe.

**Rationale:** Language analysis, and problem-solving are parts of the working memory-related task (Baddeley & Hitch, 1974). Therefore, the IOL test is chosen as a measurement to the performance of participants.

Note that the ILO is a novel test, solely measuring the participants' reasoning; thereby, advanced language skills are not required. Therefore, no language-relevant bias. Additionally, participants haven’t heard about the test (we will conduct a survey on the day of experiment), so, no prior knowledge bias.

### Secondary test: Maze Puzzle

**About the test:** Participants are given a maze, which has two doors, and exactly 1 path that connects these doors. The role of participants is finding that path.

**Rationale:** Solving a maze puzzle requires the central executive in working memory to control the information, setup goals, and planning. Therefore,  this is a perfect task for being a secondary test that distracts the participants’ working memory. 

The puzzles will be randomly generated with the same difficulty (same size, e.g 20x20) to ensure there will be no selection bias.

The readers can try to work on our sample's maze in the box at the right corner.

### Procedure

All of the participants are divided into two groups:
Group 1: Requires to do the IOL test, within 15 minutes.
Group 2: Requires to do the IOL test, with a total of 15 minutes. For every 5 minutes, they are required to solve a maze puzzle (2 in total); the time spent in solving the maze puzzle isn’t counted in the total time of solving the IOL test.
When the participants finish, their test IOL (solution:  https://ioling.org/results/best_solutions/)  score will be the main measurement for their performance. The results will be recorded by our team, ready for the discussion and conclusion.

## Variables

**Dependent variable:** The score of the IOL test.
**Independent variable:** Type: between-subjects manipulation with 2 levels — No-distraction vs Distraction (switch task every 5 min, and solve 1-min 2D maze).

# Discussion

## The capacity of working memory is limited (cognitive load)

+ [[Working Memory]] is the system for performing cognitive operations, which involves the mental presentation of information available and the maintenance of memory in the conscious mind for processing. In other words, this refers to humans' ability to hold a limited amount of mental information for processing. (Oberauer et al,2016)
+ As these two tests are conducted, switching between two cognitive-related tasks, our short-term memory has to reset to process a new task by distracting the central executive, infers to loops processing overload, which the second group’s performance might be witnessed with poorer performance than the first group. 

## Attention: Divided attention between two tasks causes lower performance
+ Attention couldn’t be sustained in the second group case is inevitable. According to Finley and his colleagues (2015), it is noticeable that using cell phone while driving contributed to factors of causing 20% injury-causing car crashes in the US in 2009. In general, this can be inferred by dividing attention impaired one or both tasks and might lead to tremendous consequences. 
+ In our experiment, the IOL test and the maze solving both require effort, thus we expect that the performance of ILO of the second group would be lower than the first group, as they have to switch to the maze puzzle every 5 minutes, causing distraction. 
+ Retroactive Inference (episodic memory) - part of attention: a type of interference in recalling memories that occurs when access to older memories is impaired by new memories. It is one common source of forgetting, particularly when the first task’s memory has not yet been consolidated but the second task has already been introduced, blocking the first one (Nieuwenstein & Wyble, 2014)
+ In this case, the maze information disrupts the IOL information in working memory: The IOL test requires participants to use rule patterns and problem solving skills in an extended amount of time. However every 5 minutes, the distraction group is required to switch to the maze puzzle, a task that requires visuospatial processing - a different skill set, forcing attentional reallocation and imposing task-switching costs.. The maze puzzle in this case can contribute to retroactive interference, thus harder to return with clarity.

# Limitations

The IOL focus on reasoning only; not reflecting the overall learning abilities of students
We are only able to test participants accuracy and the duration they're taking the test, not their reaction time
If participants are university bilingual students, results may not generalize to monolinguals, different ages, or real-world multitasking scenarios (e.g., driving + phone).

# Conclusions
In the second group, the maze puzzle introduces new skills and goals that interrupt the linguistic reasoning previously held in the working memory. This lowers their performances - lower accuracy. Therefore, the study demonstrates that multitasking can impair the required memory traces and reasoning processes for cognitive tasks. 
Hence, students shouldn’t overflow their working memory when doing cognitive-related tasks by multitasking (e.g. doing two cognitive demanding tasks at the same time). They should also avoid other distractions such as phone use, notifications, conversations during learning, as they cause retroactive interference that reduces comprehension, accuracy, and overall learning quality.
