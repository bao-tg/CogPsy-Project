# Filter Theory of Attention (Broadbent, 1958)

## Overview
The **Filter Theory of Attention**, proposed by **Donald Broadbent (1958)**, is an early **information-processing model** explaining how humans selectively attend to some sensory information while ignoring others.

It is often referred to as an **early-selection theory of attention**.

---

## Core Idea (Key Result)
Broadbent proposed that:
> **Attention acts as a selective filter that operates early in the processing stream**, allowing only certain sensory inputs to pass through for further processing based on their **physical characteristics**, not their meaning.

As a result:
- Unattended information is **blocked before semantic (meaning) processing**
- The cognitive system avoids overload by limiting what reaches conscious awareness

---

## Structure of the Model
Broadbent’s model consists of the following stages:

1. **Sensory Input**
   - Multiple stimuli enter the system simultaneously (e.g., sounds to both ears)

2. **Sensory Buffer**
   - Briefly holds all incoming sensory information
   - Very short duration (milliseconds)

3. **Selective Filter**
   - Selects information based on **physical features** such as:
     - Ear of presentation
     - Loudness
     - Pitch
   - Only one channel is selected at a time

4. **Limited-Capacity Processor**
   - Selected information is processed for meaning
   - Leads to perception, memory, and response

Unselected information **decays and is lost**.

---

## Experimental Basis
### Dichotic Listening Experiments
Broadbent’s theory was largely based on **dichotic listening tasks**, where:
- Different auditory messages are presented to each ear simultaneously
- Participants are instructed to **shadow** (repeat aloud) the message from one ear

Key findings:
- Participants could accurately shadow one ear
- They recalled little to nothing from the unattended ear
- Recall was based on **ear-by-ear**, not message meaning

---

## Implications
- Attention is **capacity-limited**
- Selection occurs **before semantic analysis**
- Explains why people miss unattended stimuli (early explanation of inattentional phenomena)
